Assessment & method

Is the FCI a leading indicator of commodity prices? Yes — and robustly. The full FCI leads broad commodity prices by about 13 months (r ≈ +0.47), and its correlation with forward commodity returns rises with horizon (+0.22 at 3 months to +0.50 at 18) — the hallmark of a structural lead rather than coincidence.

The circularity check. The FCI contains the S&P GSCI as one of its three legs, so part of that fit is mechanical. Strip the commodity leg out (dollar + rates only) and the lead survives: ~10-month peak, r ≈ +0.35, forward-return IC +0.24 to +0.37. Financial conditions genuinely lead commodities; toggle "Ex-commodities" to see the clean version.

How it's built. Each leg — DXY, S&P GSCI, UST 10-year — is taken as its deviation from a 36-month moving average (the level of tightness, which leads; raw year-on-year changes are coincident), standardized, then inverted and averaged so high = loose = growth-supportive. Read forward, it points to where commodity prices are implied to be.

Sources, sign & limitations

Sources. DXY, S&P GSCI and UST 10-year from market data (month-end); commodity price indices (IMF all-commodities, metals), year-on-year. Reconstruction of the GMI / Alpine financial-conditions lead indicator; their weights, inputs and exact lead are proprietary and differ.

Sign & current read. The FCI is currently tight — elevated commodities and yields (the growth tax) outweigh a soft-ish dollar — which points to a headwind for commodity prices roughly a year out. That is a genuinely differentiated signal: it leans the opposite way to the supply (capital-scarce) and demand (firm growth pulse) models, which is exactly why it earns a place beside them.

Limitations. Standardization is full-sample; the lead is long and the fit modest (financial conditions are one driver of the commodity cycle, not all of it); the commodity leg makes the full FCI partly self-referential, which is why the ex-commodities version is shown alongside.
